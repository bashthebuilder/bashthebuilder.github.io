The Seat He Already Had

Clacton has just spent something north of a quarter of a million pounds to elect a man who already held the job, in a contest the other parties refused to enter, six weeks after the country acquired its seventh prime minister in ten years without a single member of the public voting for him. I want to be precise about what this cost, what it bought, and why the anger underneath it is aimed at roughly the right thing for roughly the wrong reason.

14 August 2026 Politics UK Democracy Cost of living Immigration ~14 min read

This is the first MyTh about party politics, and I have hesitated over it for most of a day. I am a computer scientist, not a political scientist. I write here about research, universities and machine learning, where I can usually claim to know something. On this I am what everybody else is: a person who lives here, pays the bills, and reads the numbers.

But the numbers are the point. The house rule of this series is that every claim carries a citation, and it turns out that the ordinary British anger of August 2026 — the sense that the political class has stopped being about us — survives contact with the sources rather better than I expected, and also gets redirected by them. So: the sourced version.

What actually happened

Thirty-four candidates stood, believed to be the most in any UK parliamentary election; the ballot paper ran to 90 cm. Thirty-two of the thirty-four lost their deposits. Labour, the Conservatives, the Liberal Democrats and the Greens did not stand at all.1

Farage did not attend his own count, citing an “organised campaign to disrupt and degrade the result” and a credible threat. Essex Police said they had not advised any candidate to stay away.1

Here is the part that gets lost in the headline. His share went from 46.2% to 63.3%, which sounds like a surge and is mostly an artefact: he gained seventeen points of share by having no major opponent on the ballot. What he actually gained in human beings was 1,014 votes — 21,225 in 2024, 22,239 yesterday — on a turnout that fell from 58.7% to 44.4%.17 I want to be fair about this, because it cuts against the mockery: getting more raw votes in a by-election than in a general election, two years into the job and months into a scandal, is genuinely unusual and it is a real result. Roughly 44,400 registered electors in Clacton did not vote at all.

A man asked his constituents a question he already knew the answer to. Around 44,400 of them declined to answer it at all.

What it cost, and what it bought

Nobody publishes a definitive by-election invoice in advance. The returning officer’s costs are met from public funds, and estimates for Clacton clustered around “at least £200,000”, with the most-quoted single figure being £275,046 — the confirmed cost of the 2014 Clacton by-election uprated for inflation. The UK average is put at roughly £323,254.6 Call it a quarter of a million pounds, and understand that the precise number will be published later and will not change the argument.

What did it buy? This is where I have to be careful, because the honest answer and the satisfying answer are not the same.

The satisfying answer is that it bought a five-week holiday from scrutiny. The Parliamentary Commissioner for Standards opened a formal inquiry in May 2026 into whether Farage should have declared a £5 million gift from the British-Thai crypto billionaire Christopher Harborne, received shortly before he announced he would stand in 2024.3 When he resigned his seat on 8 July, that inquiry stopped, because the Commissioner has jurisdiction only over sitting MPs. It resumed within hours of his re-election on Friday. He will be sworn in again on 1 September.4

So the net effect of a quarter of a million pounds of public money, on the investigation, was a pause of about five weeks and a return to precisely the status quo ante.

The honest answer is that I cannot tell you that was the purpose, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Farage’s stated reason was different and, on its own terms, coherent: he called the reporting an establishment smear and said that since “the media are judging me to be a dishonest person, it is only fair to say to the voters, ‘you judge’.”23 That is a real argument. A politician accused in the press of impropriety, submitting himself to his electorate rather than to a committee, is doing something with an honourable pedigree. Bradlaugh did it. So did Tony Benn.

My objection is narrower and I think it survives. If you genuinely believe the electorate should judge you, the judgement is worth having only if it is a real contest, and this one was not: the four parties who might have made it one declined, so the referendum on Farage’s conduct was held against a man in a bin costume. And it does not resolve anything. The Commissioner is not asking whether Clacton forgives him. The Commissioner is asking whether a declaration rule was broken, which is a question of fact about a form, and no number of votes in Essex answers it. A by-election cannot acquit you of paperwork.

What is alleged, and what is established

I am going to separate these two carefully, because the loose version does nobody any good.

Alleged, and unproven. The Guardian and the Sunday Times reported between April and July 2026 that Farage failed to declare the £5m from Harborne — described by Harborne as support for Farage’s security “not just now, but for the rest of his life” — and that George Cottrell, a convicted fraudster and former UKIP fundraiser, funded elements of his 2024 operation including staffing, security and London accommodation. Questions have also been raised about donations routed via Fiona Cottrell, and about Farage lobbying the Governor of the Bank of England against a state-backed stablecoin while championing Tether, a firm in which Harborne is heavily invested. The matters sit variously with the Parliamentary Commissioner, the Electoral Commission, the Metropolitan Police and the National Crime Agency.3

Farage denies wrongdoing throughout. His position is that the Harborne money was a personal gift received before he was an MP and so not registrable, and that the Cottrell support was “totally undeclarable in every single way”; Reform UK called the Guardian’s reporting incorrect and denied Cottrell held any official role.3 None of this has been adjudicated. He may be entirely vindicated, and if he is I will say so here.

Established, and concluded. Separately, the Commissioner opened an inquiry on 30 October 2025 into Farage’s compliance with Rule 5 of the Code of Conduct. It concluded in January 2026 that he had breached the rules 17 times by registering more than £380,000 of outside earnings late — GB News payments, speaking fees, and income from the celebrity-video app Cameo. One GB News payment of £51,438 received on 29 August was not declared until 3 October. The Commissioner accepted the delays were inadvertent and attributable to staffing and administrative problems, and Farage apologised. The Commissioner also noted that intent is irrelevant to whether the rules were broken.5

That is the fair picture: one closed case of sloppiness at scale, and one open case that is serious and undecided.

The other way to take power

Now the thing I actually want to argue, which is not really about Farage at all.

While Clacton was spending a quarter of a million pounds to answer a settled question, the country changed prime minister. Keir Starmer announced on 22 June 2026 that he would resign, having lost the confidence of his MPs. Andy Burnham was nominated by 379 Labour MPs — over 94% of the parliamentary party — and when nominations closed he was the only eligible candidate. He was acclaimed leader on 17 July without a contest, and became prime minister on 20 July.8

He is the seventh prime minister in ten years. Cameron, May, Johnson, Truss, Sunak, Starmer, Burnham. Not one of the last five arrived at the job by winning a general election.

I want to be scrupulous here, because this is the point where commentary usually cheats. What happened in July is constitutionally impeccable. We do not elect prime ministers in this country; we elect a Commons, and it sustains a government. Burnham commands a majority in that Commons more comprehensively than most incoming leaders ever have. Nothing improper occurred, and people who call it a coup are wrong about how the system works.

But hold the two events next to each other, because together they say something neither says alone.

In August 2026 there were two ways to acquire power in Britain. In one, the public was asked a question whose answer was already known, at their own expense. In the other, they were not asked at all. Both were entirely legitimate. That is the problem.

This is what people are reaching for when they say politicians no longer care about them, and they are reaching badly, which is why they get told they are being unfair. The complaint is not that anyone broke the rules. It is that the machinery has arrived at a state where the formal moment of public consent and the actual transfer of power have quietly come apart — and where a genuine election, the real thing, with counts and returning officers and a 90 cm ballot paper, can be convened as a procedural instrument to serve one man’s position. Both halves are legal. Neither half is a democracy behaving well.

Jaywick

Inside the Clacton constituency, past the pier, there is a neighbourhood called Jaywick — the Brooklands estate and the Jaywick Sands promenade, much of it a former holiday park that people ended up living in permanently.

The English Indices of Deprivation rank 33,755 neighbourhoods in England. Jaywick has been ranked the single most deprived of them in 2010, 2015, 2019 and again in the 2025 edition published on 30 October 2025. Four times in a row, across fifteen years, five prime ministers, and three governing arrangements. The UN’s special rapporteur on extreme poverty went there on a fact-finding mission to Britain.9

Asked about it on the day the 2025 figures landed, Farage said he was “obviously sad that things aren’t improving more quickly”, that “there’s a limit to what one person can do”, and that “everybody just feels very, very burdened these days.”10

I am not going to be cheap about that, because the last of those three sentences is true and is the most honest thing in this entire essay. But set the middle sentence beside the sums involved. There is a limit to what one person can do for the most deprived neighbourhood in England. There was, apparently, no limit on £5 million for one person’s security. There was no limit on a quarter of a million pounds of public money for one person’s mandate. Tendring District Council’s regeneration plan for Jaywick Sands is costed at £126 million and will take a generation.9

And the man who represents it has, since July 2024, been criticised for one of the weakest voting attendance records in the Commons — monitors have logged long unbroken runs of absences, including a stretch of 77 consecutive divisions missed — and for holding no in-person constituency surgeries. Different trackers give different totals depending on the window and the definition, so take the precise figure with care; the direction is not in dispute.11

The audacity argument

Here is the argument I hear most often from people who feel the way I do about the last decade, and which I have some sympathy with even though I think it is wrong: what Britain lacks is nerve. Someone who will stand in front of the country and say the hard thing about the boats, about immigration, about how fast this place has changed — and not flinch. Someone with Trump’s audacity, whatever you think of Trump.

I understand the appeal completely. And it happens to be an argument with a testable claim inside it, so let us test it.

The audacity thesis says that on immigration and the Channel, timid managerial politics has failed and only confrontation will move the numbers. But the numbers have moved, a great deal, and not because anyone was audacious:

  • Small boat crossings are down 43% year on year. Roughly 14,526 arrivals to 2 August 2026, against 25,436 over the same period in 2025. The first half of 2026 was down 41% on the first half of 2025, and 12% below the equivalent 2024 figure — the largest sustained reduction since the data began in 2018.12
  • Net migration has roughly halved, twice. It peaked at 906,000 in the year to June 2023. For the year ending December 2025 it was 171,000 — down 48% on 2024, and the lowest since 2012 outside the pandemic. Work arrivals fell 47% in a single year.13

That is an enormous change, delivered under two governments of different parties, by visa rules, a student-dependants ban, enforcement co-operation and paperwork. It is the least charismatic possible mechanism. Nobody got a standing ovation for it. And the theory that only a strongman could shift these numbers has, in the plainest way available, just lost its bet.

Let me give the other side its strongest form, because I do not think the argument dies there. You can reply: fine, but the composition and the pace of the last decade’s arrivals were never consented to at any election, and pointing at an improving derivative does not settle a question about the level. That is a serious objection and I am not going to wave it away. The honest answer is that it is an argument about what kind of country people want, which is legitimately political and cannot be settled by a Home Office statistical release — but it also cannot be settled by audacity, because audacity is a personal quality, not a policy. And on the specific empirical claim actually being made — that only confrontation moves the numbers — the evidence is now in and it says otherwise.

There is also a harder thing to say to anyone who wants a British Trump specifically. The reason Britain has had seven prime ministers in ten years is that our system removes leaders who lose their party’s confidence, quickly and without ceremony. That is the same instability people are furious about. It is also the only reason a British leader cannot ignore a rule and dare anyone to stop him. You cannot have the disposal mechanism and the strongman; they are the same lever pointing in opposite directions. Wishing for one is wishing away the other.

What the anger is actually about

I arrived in this country in 2015, to a postdoctoral post at Cardiff. Eleven years. I remember thinking that things here basically worked, in an unglamorous way, and that this was underrated. I do not think that now, and I have spent some time trying to work out whether that is Britain changing or me ageing.

The data says it is mostly Britain, and it says the thing people are angry about is not chiefly immigration at all:

  • Headline CPI inflation was 2.6% in the year to June 2026, close to target and falling.14 This is the statistic ministers reach for, and it is the least informative one available, because a falling rate of increase is not a fall.
  • The Bank of England’s 30 July forecast has inflation rising back to 3.2% by October and staying above 3% until the third quarter of 2027.14 The squeeze is not over; it has a scheduled second act.
  • The Joseph Rowntree Foundation puts 7.4 million low-income families unable to afford essentials this year — the highest since its tracker began in 2021.15
  • In April 2026, 92% of Great Britain households reporting a rising cost of living named food as a component; the cumulative rises since 2021 on staples are of a different order from the headline rate — analysis by the Energy and Climate Intelligence Unit puts eggs up around 59% and beef around 64% over that period.16

That gap — between a headline rate a minister can defend and a shopping basket a household actually carries — is where the rage lives. And it is politically homeless. No party owns it, because no party has a plausible answer to it, and so it gets displaced onto the one subject where somebody is always willing to offer you a villain. That is my actual objection to the audacity politics: not that it is nasty, but that it is a redirection. It takes an unbearable and legitimate grievance about money and rehouses it as a grievance about people, where nothing can be solved and the anger is renewable.

Meanwhile the polling shows the thing nobody expected. Reform is not surging; it is roughly where the other two are and, on some measures, sliding. Mid-August 2026 has Labour 24–28%, Reform 22–25%, the Conservatives 20–21%; the YouGov reading of 9–10 August put Reform on 22%, its joint-lowest since that series restarted in January 2025.17 Three parties inside six points of each other, and none of them above 28%. That is not a country realigning behind an insurgent. That is a country that has withdrawn its confidence from everybody, which is a more dangerous condition and a much harder one to fix.

The bin was the argument

Count Binface took 9,455 votes and 26.93% — his best result in any parliamentary election, and about one in four of everyone who turned out.1 He was second. The story went round the world.

It is tempting to file that under British eccentricity, and I think that would be a mistake. Nine and a half thousand adults, in the most deprived corner of England, walked to a polling station on a Thursday in August and used the only instrument they had. Nobody does that for a joke. They did it because the serious parties had vacated the field, and when the ballot offers you no way to say this whole exercise is contemptuous of us, people will find one. The costume was the message and the message was legible.

Three things I think I can defend, and none of them are comfortable.

The procedural anger is correct and the target is wrong. Something genuinely broke in the last decade: a general election is no longer where the country’s leadership is decided, and an actual election can be convened as a personal instrument. But this is a defect of the machinery, not of any individual’s character, and every time it gets discussed as a story about one man’s ego it becomes unfixable, because it is not a story about ego. It is a story about a system with no rule requiring the public to be consulted when power changes hands, and no rule stopping a by-election being used as a shield.

Wanting audacity is wanting the wrong tool. On the two issues most often cited, the numbers have already moved further and faster than a decade of confrontational rhetoric managed, by unglamorous administrative means, under governments of both colours.1213 What Britain is short of is not nerve. It is anyone willing to say that the cost-of-living crisis has not ended, has a second act scheduled for this October, and cannot be blamed on a boat.

And I should hold my own side to this. The comfortable version of this essay, from someone with my job, is that Clacton voted badly and needs educating. It did not and it does not. Clacton has been the most deprived place in England through four consecutive measurements and five prime ministers, and no one who could have fixed that has. When such a place returns the man who at least turns up rhetorically, and gives a quarter of its votes to a bin, that is not stupidity. It is an accurate report on what has been on offer. The failure being described is ours before it is theirs.

I would rather be corrected on any of this than agreed with lazily. Everything above is sourced; if a number is wrong, tell me which one.
